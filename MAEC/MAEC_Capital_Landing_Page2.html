1.1 The Great Misconception About Money

Most people — including many finance ministers and treasury officials — operate under a fundamental misunderstanding: Money is a fixed thing. Banks lend out deposited money. Governments can only spend collected taxes. To get a loan, you need assets worth at least as much as the loan.

Every part of this is incorrect. Economies that understand this fact have built highways, railways, and technology industries. Those that cling to the misunderstanding are still waiting for donor grants.

1.2 How Money Is Created — Real Examples

Kansas City Municipal Bond: The city issued a USD 800M revenue bond backed by future sewer fees. The money did not exist before the bond was issued. It was created by the promise of future revenue.

US Federal Reserve QE: After 2008, the Fed purchased USD 3.7 trillion in bonds. It did not have this cash. It created the money digitally — typing new numbers into accounts — backed by future mortgage repayments and tax revenues.

Wall Street Derivatives: The global derivatives market exceeds USD 600 trillion — roughly six times global GDP. None exists as physical money. It is all promises, secured by future value and institutional credibility.

China's Infrastructure: China built more high-speed rail (2008–2020) than the rest of the world combined. It paid via Revenue Rights Asset-Backed Securities — promises backed by future toll revenues.

Bitcoin Mining: A Texas mining company with no revenue raised USD 500M by issuing tokens representing claims on future Bitcoin production.

The pattern is universal: Every sophisticated economy creates money from future value. MAEC Capital brings this exact capability to the developing world.

1.3 The Economist Who Proved It

Professor Richard Werner — who coined 'Quantitative Easing' — co-authored research with the Bank of England in 2014. The Bank's official conclusion:

"Banks do not lend out money that has been deposited with them. When a bank makes a loan, it creates a deposit in the borrower's account, effectively creating new money."

Your country's banks are not short of money. They are short of confidence, understanding, and the credit enhancement instruments that allow them to deploy their lending capacity safely. MAEC provides those instruments.

3.1 The Letter of Credit — Starting with the Familiar

An LC is a payment instrument. A company in Lagos orders machinery from Germany. The Lagos bank issues an LC — a formal promise that if the machinery arrives and documents are correct, the bank will pay. It substitutes the bank's creditworthiness for the importer's.

3.2 The SBLC — A Different Animal Entirely

A Standby Letter of Credit (SBLC) serves a fundamentally different purpose. The word 'Standby' is the key. It stands by. It waits. It is a contingent commitment — a formal assurance that if a defined trigger condition occurs, the bank will perform.

Metaphor: An LC is like a taxi from A to B. An SBLC is like a rescue helicopter on standby. You hope you never need it. But its mere presence gives confidence that the operation can proceed safely.

In the MAEC framework, the SBLC is a credit enhancement instrument — not a payment guarantee. It transforms a project from 'unrated and uninvestable' to 'credit-enhanced and bankable.'

3.4 Why 'Cash Backed' Does Not Mean Blocked Cash

'Cash backed' means the SBLC is secured by near-cash assets — assets with defined, contractually certain future cash value:

  • Future PPA revenues — government utilities have contractual obligations to pay defined tariffs
  • Future toll revenues — legally defined, traffic-count supported, regulator-approved income
  • Future mineral royalty streams — legislatively mandated payments from concession holders
  • PRBS certificates representing hypothecated future income

Under UCP 600 and ISP98, an SBLC is independent of underlying contracts. The backing can be, and routinely is, future project revenue.

3.5 Why the Bank Does Not Need to Block Cash

The bank's credit officer says: 'We cannot issue an SBLC of EUR 100M without blocking EUR 100M in cash.' This is wrong for four reasons:

  • Basel III CCF: A performance-contingent SBLC attracts a 50% Credit Conversion Factor, not 100%. The bank needs capital equivalent to 50% of risk-weighted exposure.
  • Off-Balance-Sheet: Under IAS 37 and IFRS 7, the SBLC is a contingent liability disclosed in Notes, not on the balance sheet face.
  • PRBS Asset: The PRBS certificate held as Held-to-Maturity under IFRS 9 creates a positive balance sheet entry. Net effect: neutral to positive.
  • The Indemnity: MAEC executes a legally binding Indemnity confirming no claim will ever be made against the SBLC.

3.6 The Single Obligor Limit — A Non-Issue

Because SBLCs are off-balance-sheet contingent instruments, not direct loans, they do not count toward the Single Obligor Limit (typically 25% of Tier 1 capital) in most regulatory frameworks.

4.1 Why the Indemnity Exists

Even sophisticated bankers have residual concerns: what if something goes wrong? What if someone draws on the SBLC improperly? The Indemnity Agreement transfers risk away from the issuing bank and onto MAEC.

4.2 What the Indemnity Does

  • Indemnifies the bank from any negative occurrence to the SBLC MT760 instrument
  • Explicitly confirms the SBLC is for credit enhancement only; MAEC will never draw on it
  • Protects the bank from third-party claims arising from the SBLC's use
  • Remains in effect for the SBLC's validity period
  • Provides for MAEC to participate in the bank's defence at MAEC's expense
  • Governed by ICC Paris or Swiss Court trade law

4.3 The Temporal Firewall

The SBLC is valid for 1 year and 1 day. The MAEC loan's grace period is 3 to 5 years. Repayment begins only after the grace period. The SBLC expires before any repayment obligation falls due. It is structurally impossible to claim against the SBLC based on project performance.

5.1 Hypothecation — The Oldest Trick in Finance

Hypothecation comes from Greek hypotheke, meaning 'pledge.' A debtor pledges an asset as security but retains possession and use. Your mortgage is a hypothecation — you pledge your house but continue living in it.

MAEC applies this to future project revenue. A government hypothecates future toll revenues, PPA revenues, or mineral royalties — pledging the right to receive them as security while retaining operational control.

5.2 The PRBS — From Future Revenue to Present Capital

The Project Revenue-Backed Security (PRBS) converts hypothecated future revenue into a bankable asset. Like a land title certifies physical land ownership, a PRBS certifies ownership of a future revenue stream.

Metaphor: A farmer will harvest 10,000 tonnes of coffee next year. A merchant pays today for the right to collect it later. The Coffee Revenue Certificate is the PRBS.

MAEC PRBS Process:

  • Future revenues assigned to SPV via Revenue Assignment Deed
  • SPV issues PRBS Certificate representing beneficial interest
  • Local bank holds PRBS as Held-to-Maturity Debt Security (IFRS 9) — a balance sheet asset
  • Bank issues SBLC backed by the PRBS
  • MAEC's platform receives the SBLC, completes enhancement, and deploys the loan
  • Project builds, revenues commence, waterfall repays the loan

7.1 The Question

"If MAEC has access to HSBC, Barclays, BNP Paribas, and ICBC, why does it need a small bank in Lusaka or Kampala to issue an SBLC first?"

7.2 Reason 1: Regulatory Compliance

MAEC is not licensed in your country. It cannot legally deploy capital directly without routing through the regulated banking system. The local bank's SBLC is the regulatory bridge connecting MAEC's international platform to your domestic economy.

7.3 Reason 2: Risk Transfer

MAEC's partners do not take uncollateralised credit risk in unfamiliar markets. The local bank's SBLC transforms 'uncollateralised loan to an African project' into 'loan secured by SBLC from a regulated local bank, enhanced by MAEC' — which credit committees can approve.

7.4 Reason 3: Leverage

MAEC enhances a non-rated SBLC (10–15% LTV on its own) to 100% LTV through insurance (Lloyd's of London, Redbridge), PRBS collateral recognition, structured credit engineering, and underlying project revenue assignment.

7.5 Reason 4: Proof of Local Commitment

The local bank's SBLC signals that a regulated, supervised institution has assessed and stands behind the transaction — like management co-investing in private equity.

12.1 The Donor Model Is Exhausted

For 60 years, developing world infrastructure has been financed through sovereign borrowing (at rates that destroy fiscal space), donor grants (tied, slow, insufficient), and World Bank/IMF loans (conditional, intrusive, limited).

These channels will not build the railways, hospitals, power grids, and smart cities the next generation deserves. The Africa Infrastructure Deficit alone is USD 100 billion per year. World Bank and AfDB combined disburse USD 40 billion globally for all sectors. Donor aid is a rounding error.

The capital needed is in global markets — pension funds in Norway, Japan, Canada; sovereign wealth funds in Abu Dhabi and Singapore; institutional investors seeking stable, long-dated, yield-generating assets. Solar plants in Zimbabwe and toll roads in Botswana, backed by government revenue assignments and bank credit enhancement, are exactly what they seek.

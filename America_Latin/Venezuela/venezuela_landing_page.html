Strategic Partnership Proposal

Venezuelan Comprehensive Economic Development Funding

A €300 billion multi-phase development program to transform Venezuela's natural-resource wealth into sustainable industrial production, exports, employment, and government revenues.

01

Executive Investment Thesis

Venezuela possesses an extraordinary combination of assets: the world's largest proven crude-oil reserves, very substantial natural-gas reserves, large iron-ore and bauxite resources, significant gold potential, and a Caribbean location strategically positioned between North American, Latin American, European and Atlantic markets.

The €300 billion platform should be sequenced around: Asset recovery → Energy expansion → Refining → Petrochemicals → Fertilizers → Metals → Manufacturing → Export diversification.

The financing case rests on the ability to convert resources into bankable cash-flow-generating industrial assets, not the nominal value of resources in the ground.

03

Industrial Development Program

The program is organized around five industrial corridors: Orinoco Industrial Corridor (oil, gas, iron, steel, aluminium, mining, power); Paraguaná Refining & Petrochemical Corridor (refining, crude upgrading, fuels, aromatics, olefins, polymers); José–Anzoátegui Petrochemical Corridor (methanol, ammonia, urea, polymers, industrial chemicals); Zulia–Western Energy & Industrial Corridor (oil, gas, fertilizer, petrochemicals, agriculture); and Caribbean Export Corridor (ports, storage, refining products, LNG/gas products, international shipping).

04

Refining & Petrochemicals

Venezuela's refineries were operating at approximately 35% of their combined 1.29 million barrels/day nameplate capacity in February 2026, processing around 450,000 barrels/day. The first investment should be rehabilitation and debottlenecking, not immediate abandonment of existing assets.

Proposed Refinery No. 1: 300,000–400,000 bpd full-conversion coking/hydrocracking refinery on the Caribbean coast, with atmospheric/vacuum distillation, delayed coking, hydrocracking, diesel hydrotreating, catalytic reforming, alkylation, sulfur recovery, and hydrogen production.

Proposed Refinery No. 2: Second 300,000–400,000 bpd refinery in the eastern Venezuelan industrial corridor, subject to crude availability and environmental permits. Combined potential: 600,000–800,000 bpd of new capacity.

The refinery should become the first stage of a broader carbon-and-hydrocarbon value chain: Crude → Refinery → Naphtha/LPG/Ethane/Propane/Aromatics → Ethylene/Propylene/BTX → Polyethylene/Polypropylene/PET/Styrenics → Industrial Manufacturing.

05

Fertilizer Strategy

Can Venezuela produce urea at $180/tonne? Potentially as a production-cost target under favorable gas and plant-efficiency assumptions; not as a guaranteed international export selling price.

Urea economics are dominated by natural-gas costs. At a gas price of approximately $7/MMBtu, gas-related costs alone can be a very large component of total urea cash cost. Current international prices are materially above $180/t — Middle East granular urea benchmarked at approximately $400/t in Q1 2025, with 2026 market intelligence placing Middle East granular urea around $268/t in Q2 2026 and much higher delivered prices in destination markets.

The strategic target should be: Low-cost production, not artificially low selling price. A credible long-term objective is to investigate whether Venezuela can achieve approximately $150–220/tonne cash-cost economics for urea under low-cost domestic-gas scenarios, while underwriting the project against a substantially wider range of market prices.

Venezuela already has demonstrated fertilizer infrastructure. Pequiven reported historical operation of an ammonia plant at approximately 1,500 tonnes/day and a urea plant at approximately 2,200 tonnes/day at the José Antonio Anzoátegui complex.

06

€300 Billion Funding Framework

The €300 billion should be regarded as a maximum program envelope, not money to be drawn immediately. The program should be structured as a portfolio of approximately 50–100 bankable projects and project clusters, with financing released only after technical and financial milestones.

The principal recommendation is that Venezuela should not finance the entire €300 billion as conventional sovereign debt. Instead: Project Finance with SPVs, Joint Ventures, Export-Credit Financing, Development Finance, Strategic Offtake Financing, and Asset-Backed Structures.

Economic Impact

The program should be evaluated through measurable indicators rather than political promises. The World Bank reports that Venezuela's GDP was approximately €/$100 billion-equivalent in 2025 in current U.S.-dollar terms, with real GDP growth of approximately 1.6%. The IMF's April 2026 database projects nominal GDP of approximately $111.3 billion in 2026.

Against this economic base, €300 billion is enormous. It cannot responsibly be deployed rapidly. It should be treated as a multi-cycle capital platform, with each subsequent phase dependent upon demonstrated performance of the preceding phase.

08

Risk Mitigation

Sanctions & Geopolitical Risk: The U.S. Treasury's OFAC Venezuela program currently contains multiple general licenses. Every transaction must undergo OFAC screening, EU sanctions review, beneficial-owner screening, and correspondent-bank review. No financing should close merely on the assumption that sanctions will remain relaxed.

Commodity Price Risk: Oil, gas, urea, metals and petrochemical prices are cyclical. Projects must be stress-tested under low/base/high price scenarios, currency depreciation, and export disruption.

Construction Risk: Every major EPC project should include fixed-price or appropriately structured EPC contracts, performance guarantees, liquidated damages, completion tests, independent engineer certification, and contingency reserves.

Currency Risk: Revenue and debt should be matched wherever possible. Export-dollar revenue should service dollar/euro-linked debt rather than bolívar revenue servicing foreign-currency debt.

Environmental Risk: Oil, mining and heavy industry require rigorous environmental governance including baseline studies, water-management plans, tailings management, methane reduction, refinery emissions controls, sulfur recovery, mine rehabilitation, biodiversity protection, and independent monitoring.

09

Governance Architecture

We recommend creation of a Venezuela National Industrial Development & Investment Authority with an independent technical and financial board. The Authority would establish: Investment Committee (project approval), Technical Committee (engineering and feasibility), Financial Committee (debt capacity and financial models), Environmental & Social Committee (ESG safeguards), Procurement Committee (transparent procurement), and Independent Audit Function (annual financial and project audits).

Each project should have a dedicated SPV and ring-fenced accounts. The non-sovereign financing principle ensures that government retains policy and regulatory oversight while project cash flows service project debt.
